In the autumn of 1913, two radical political currents flared simultaneously on opposite sides of the Atlantic: the Dublin Lockout, led by Jim Larkin and James Connolly's Irish Citizen Army, and the founding of the Ghadar Party by Indian expatriates in San Francisco and Astoria, Oregon.

Though separated by geography, the organizers maintained direct links. Irish republican newspapers reprinted manifestos from Indian revolutionaries, while Indian students in Dublin and London regularly attended Irish nationalist gatherings.

## Shared Printing Presses & Underground Networks

During the First World War, networks like the Friends of Freedom for India in the United States worked intimately with Clan na Gael. Figures like Lala Har Dayal, Taraknath Das, and Liam Mellows coordinated anti-imperial propaganda and legal defenses.

When Terence MacSwiney, Lord Mayor of Cork, died on hunger strike in Brixton Prison in 1920, the news shook India. Mass meetings were held in Calcutta, Bombay, and Lahore, and Jawaharlal Nehru later reflected that MacSwiney's sacrifice served as an enduring inspiration for Indian political prisoners.

## Mutual Recognition and Early Diplomatic Solidarity

When Dáil Éireann established its diplomatic mission in the United States and Paris, Indian delegates were frequent guests. The mutual recognition established during these perilous decades laid the groundwork for immediate diplomatic relations when both republics achieved sovereignty.
